Skilled Migration Consultant in Perth: Understanding SkillSelect, Skills Assessments and State Nomination

Published September 7, 2026 | Category: Skilled Migration

Skilled Migration

Australia's skilled migration program is designed to attract people with qualifications and experience that can contribute to the Australian workforce.

For applicants, however, the skilled migration process can appear complicated.

Terms such as skills assessment, ANZSCO, SkillSelect, Expression of Interest, subclass 189, subclass 190, subclass 491, state nomination and points test are often discussed together.

A Skilled Migration Consultant in Perth can help applicants understand how these different components connect and whether skilled migration is realistic for their circumstances.

What Is Skilled Migration?

Skilled migration refers to visa pathways intended for eligible skilled workers.

The best-known points-tested pathways include:

  • Skilled Independent visa subclass 189
  • Skilled Nominated visa subclass 190
  • Skilled Work Regional (Provisional) visa subclass 491

These pathways are managed through SkillSelect.

Home Affairs describes SkillSelect as the government's online system through which skilled workers submit an Expression of Interest to indicate that they want to be considered for skilled migration.

Submitting an EOI is not the same as submitting a visa application.

For relevant points-tested visas, an applicant must first receive an invitation before lodging the visa application.

Step 1: Identify Your Occupation

The first major question is usually:

"What is my nominated occupation?"

Australia maintains skilled occupation lists that connect occupations with relevant visa pathways and assessing authorities.

Applicants should not select an occupation based solely on the job title printed on their employment contract.

Migration assessments can consider the actual nature of an applicant's employment, qualifications and duties.

For example, two people with the title "manager" may perform very different roles and may not qualify for the same nominated occupation.

This is one reason a Skilled Migration Consultant in Perth may review employment duties before recommending a skills assessment pathway.

Step 2: Complete the Relevant Skills Assessment

A suitable skills assessment is a key requirement for many skilled migration applicants.

Home Affairs states that applicants for points-tested subclass 189, subclass 190 and subclass 491 generally need a suitable assessment for their nominated occupation and must have obtained it by the relevant stage of the invitation process.

Skills assessments are conducted by authorised assessing authorities.

Requirements differ between occupations.

Depending on the profession, applicants may need to demonstrate:

  • Academic qualifications
  • Employment experience
  • Specific coursework
  • Professional registration
  • Practical experience
  • English-language ability
  • Employment evidence

The assessment process should therefore be reviewed before assuming that a qualification automatically qualifies a person for migration.

Step 3: Calculate Your Skilled Migration Points

The points test plays a major role in subclass 189, 190 and 491 pathways.

Points may be available for factors including:

  • Age
  • English language proficiency
  • Skilled employment
  • Qualifications
  • Australian study
  • Partner qualifications
  • State nomination
  • Eligible regional factors

The current minimum threshold is 65 points for these points-tested pathways, but Home Affairs expressly notes that achieving the threshold does not guarantee an invitation.

An applicant should therefore distinguish between:

Eligibility score: whether you can potentially enter the process.

and

Competitive score: whether your EOI is sufficiently competitive within the current migration environment.

A professional Australia PR Consultant Perth can help applicants understand this difference.

Step 4: Submit an Expression of Interest

Once key requirements are addressed, an applicant may submit an EOI through SkillSelect.

The EOI includes information about the applicant's:

  • Age
  • Education
  • Employment
  • English ability
  • Skills assessment
  • Points
  • Preferred skilled visa pathways

Applicants must ensure information in the EOI is accurate.

If circumstances change, relevant details should be reviewed and updated where appropriate.

Home Affairs has advised applicants to keep EOIs current and accurate, particularly in advance of invitation rounds.

Understanding Subclass 189

Subclass 189 is the Skilled Independent visa.

It is attractive to many applicants because state nomination is not required.

However, invitation rounds are competitive.

Home Affairs ranks EOIs based on factors including points, with higher-ranked EOIs generally receiving priority within applicable invitation arrangements.

Applicants should therefore avoid assuming that submitting an EOI guarantees an invitation.

Understanding Subclass 190

Subclass 190 is a state-nominated permanent skilled visa.

Applicants must satisfy federal visa requirements and obtain nomination from an Australian state or territory.

States have their own criteria and workforce priorities.

Home Affairs explains that state and territory governments assess candidates based on criteria specific to their jurisdiction.

For applicants working with a Migration Agent in Perth, Western Australian nomination may be one of the options considered.

However, state nomination policies should always be checked under current program requirements.

Understanding Subclass 491

Subclass 491 is a provisional regional skilled visa.

It may involve nomination by a state or territory government or eligible family sponsorship, depending on the stream.

Home Affairs currently describes the visa as allowing eligible skilled workers to live and work in designated regional Australia for five years and potentially apply for permanent residency through subclass 191 if eligible.

Applicants considering subclass 491 should understand the regional obligations associated with the visa before proceeding.

How State Nomination Changes Skilled Migration

State nomination can create additional opportunities, but it also introduces another set of criteria.

States may prioritise factors such as:

  • Occupation
  • Employment
  • Residence
  • Qualification
  • English ability
  • Work experience
  • Industry demand

These settings may change between program years.

Applicants should therefore avoid relying on screenshots, old social media videos or eligibility lists from previous years.

An Australia Immigration Consultant in Perth should refer to current requirements when assessing a potential state-nominated pathway.

Is 65 Points Enough?

Technically, 65 is the minimum threshold currently specified for relevant points-tested visas.

Practically, an invitation may require a more competitive profile.

The points required can vary depending on occupation, invitation round and migration program demand.

Therefore, rather than asking only:

"Do I have 65 points?"

Ask:

"What can I legitimately do to strengthen my migration profile?"

Depending on the applicant, possibilities might include:

  • Improving English results
  • Gaining additional eligible work experience
  • Completing recognised qualifications
  • Exploring state nomination
  • Considering regional options
  • Reviewing partner points

Every strategy must be assessed according to current eligibility rules.

Skilled Migration After Australian Study

International graduates commonly explore skilled migration after completing an Australian qualification.

Some may first become eligible for a Temporary Graduate visa subclass 485.

The 485 visa provides temporary stay and work rights for eligible Australian graduates, but it is not itself a guarantee of permanent residency.

This is why students seeking an Australia Student Visa Consultant Perth should also understand the difference between selecting an educational pathway and qualifying for skilled migration.

A course should never be marketed as automatic PR.

Employer Sponsorship as an Alternative

Not every applicant will obtain an invitation through SkillSelect.

For some skilled workers, an employer-sponsored route may become relevant.

An Employer Sponsored Visa Australia pathway may involve visas such as subclass 482, subclass 186 or subclass 494.

Home Affairs currently describes the Skills in Demand subclass 482 as an employer-sponsored temporary skilled visa, subclass 186 as a permanent employer nomination option and subclass 494 as a regional employer-sponsored provisional option.

An Australia Work Visa Consultant can review whether employer sponsorship deserves consideration alongside skilled migration.

Documentation Matters

Migration claims generally need supporting evidence.

Applicants should maintain organised records of:

  • Qualifications
  • Academic transcripts
  • English-language results
  • Employment contracts
  • Reference letters
  • Payslips
  • Tax records
  • Professional registration
  • Skills assessments
  • Identity documents

Waiting until receiving an invitation to organise several years of employment evidence can create unnecessary pressure.

Good document management should begin early.

Avoid Common Skilled Migration Mistakes

Common mistakes include:

Selecting the wrong occupation
A similar job title does not necessarily mean the occupation is suitable.

Claiming points without evidence
Points claimed in an EOI must be supportable.

Assuming 65 points guarantees an invitation
It does not.

Using an expired or unsuitable skills assessment
Applicants should check the validity and requirements relevant to their circumstances.

Depending on outdated state criteria
State nomination programs change.

Following another applicant's pathway blindly
Two people working in the same industry may have different age, qualifications, English scores and employment histories.

Australia's skilled migration system rewards applicants who meet specific requirements, not applicants who simply follow popular trends.

Your occupation, age, English results, qualifications, experience, skills assessment and nomination opportunities all matter.

A carefully prepared migration strategy should therefore answer:

  • Which occupation fits my background?
  • Can I obtain a suitable skills assessment?
  • How many eligible points do I have?
  • Which visas can I realistically consider?
  • Does state nomination make sense?
  • Are regional opportunities relevant?
  • Should employer sponsorship also be explored?
